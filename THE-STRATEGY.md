### How to use this document

This is not a book to read once. It is a working manual with templates you copy, numbers you check against your own, and scripts you send. The parts are ordered by dependency: nothing in Part Three works until Part Two is settled, and nothing in Part Seven works unless Part Six was done properly.

Everything here is built on two facts you established, and they are the only two facts that matter:

- You have **under five hours a week.** Not "under five on a bad week." Under five as the baseline, because school is the job and this is the side thing.
- You have **a route to invoicing that exists but is not set up.** Possible, not done.

Those two constraints eliminate about ninety percent of the advice that exists on the internet about making money as a young person. They eliminate freelance marketplaces, which pay for hours you do not have. They eliminate content creation, which pays only after hundreds of unpaid hours. They eliminate anything requiring you to be online at predictable times.

What survives is narrow and it is good: **build a thing once, sell it repeatedly, and attach a monthly fee to it.**

#### The four rules this document keeps returning to

1. Never sell hours. Sell a defined outcome at a fixed price.
2. Never quote the monthly fee after the build. Quote it in the same breath as the build price.
3. Never accept open scope. Two revision rounds, written down, everything else quoted separately.
4. Never start a second product before the first one has been sold twice.

> **The honest ceiling**
>
> At four hours a week, a realistic target is a few hundred euros a month within six to twelve months, with the monthly fees doing most of the work by month nine. Anyone selling you a bigger number at this time budget is selling you a course, not a business.

### The one-page version

If you read nothing else, read this page. Everything after it is the detail.

#### Where the money actually is

Your Skroutz shop sells power strips, travel adapters and Brennenstuhl products. That is a commodity category on a price-comparison engine, which is the single worst margin structure that exists — the buyer sorts by price, the SKU is identical everywhere, and you are bidding against Public, Plaisio and Kotsovolos, all of whom buy at volumes you cannot approach. Every euro you earn there is earned against a headwind.

Your web work has the opposite structure. The Kapriol build required you to solve a genuinely hard problem: turning a supplier's catalogue PDF into structured product JSON and then into a fast static site. That problem exists at hundreds of distributors across Greece and the Balkans, almost none of whom can solve it, and the solution does not get cheaper for a competitor just because they have more capital.

#### The asset you own

It is not the code. It is not HTML skill, which is common. It is the **pipeline** — catalogue PDF in, working product site out — and the fact that you have run it end to end once already. The second time you run it, it takes a third as long. That gap between what you charge and what it costs you in hours is the entire business.

#### The sequence

| Step | What it is | Time cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Resolve the invoicing route | 2–3 hours, once |
| 2 | Package Kapriol as a portfolio piece | 2 hours, once |
| 3 | Set a price and write it down | 1 hour, once |
| 4 | Land one client | 4–8 hours of outreach |
| 5 | Build, with fixed scope | 10–15 hours |
| 6 | Convert to a monthly fee | said at step 4, not step 6 |
| 7 | Repeat step 4 | forever |

Steps 1 through 3 total about six hours. They are worth more than the next hundred hours of building, because right now everything you can do is unmonetisable — not for lack of skill, but for lack of a way to take money and a number to put on a page.

## Part One — The Strategic Position

### 1. Why the web work beats the shop

Two businesses can both make money and still be completely different animals. The distinction that matters is not how much each earns per month. It is what happens when you add one more unit of effort.

#### Marginal economics

On Skroutz, selling one more power strip requires you to have bought that power strip. Your earnings scale with capital deployed, and your margin is set by a market you do not control. If you want to double revenue you must roughly double inventory, which means doubling money tied up in boxes. Your effort per sale does not go down much either — orders still need handling, stock still needs counting.

On a catalogue site, selling one more build requires you to have the pipeline, which you already have. Your earnings scale with client relationships, and your margin is set by what the client believes the outcome is worth, not by what a comparison engine displays. If you want to double revenue you find one more client. The effort per build falls each time, because you are re-running a process rather than inventing one.

| Dimension | Skroutz shop | Catalogue sites |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What limits growth | Capital | Client relationships |
| Who sets your price | The comparison engine | You and the client |
| Cost of one more unit | The wholesale cost | Almost nothing |
| Effort per unit over time | Roughly flat | Falls steeply |
| Fits four hours a week? | Only if lean | Yes, by design |
| Produces recurring income? | No | Yes, via retainer |

*The two businesses, side by side. Note the last row — it is the one that decides this.*

#### The point about price-setting

This is the part people miss. On Skroutz your price is discovered by an algorithm and displayed next to eleven competitors offering the identical item. There is no argument you can make for charging more. The product is the same product.

For a catalogue site, the client is not comparing you to eleven identical offers, because there are not eleven people who will turn their 200-page supplier PDF into a working site. They are comparing you to: doing nothing, hiring an agency at four to eight times your price, or asking a cousin who will not finish. That comparison set is enormously favourable to you.

> **The pricing insight**
>
> You are not competing on being cheap. You are competing on being the only person who will actually deliver. Price accordingly, and never lead with the fact that you are fast — clients read speed as "this must be easy" and adjust the price they expect downward.

#### What this does not mean

It does not mean shut the shop. The shop is an existing, functioning source of income with money already deployed in it. Closing a working thing to chase a better thing is how people end up with neither.

It means: stop spending your scarce hours trying to grow the shop, run it in maintenance mode, and put every hour of genuine attention into the web side. Part Ten covers exactly what maintenance mode looks like and the one adjustment worth making to the shop's inventory.

### 2. What four hours a week forbids

Time constraints do not just make things slower. Past a certain point they make entire business models impossible, because some models have a fixed overhead that eats a small budget completely.

#### The overhead problem

Every client relationship carries costs that have nothing to do with building: reading and answering messages, clarifying what they meant, chasing a logo file, chasing payment, explaining why the thing they asked for on Tuesday changes the thing they approved on Monday. Call this the relationship overhead.

Relationship overhead is roughly constant per client. A €400 client and a €900 client generate about the same volume of email. This has a brutal implication at your time budget:

> **The arithmetic**
>
> If relationship overhead is two hours a month per client, then five clients consume ten hours a month — more than half your entire budget — before you have built anything at all. Two clients consume four hours a month and leave you room to work.

So the strategy is not "get as many clients as possible." It is **few clients, high prices, low-touch maintenance.** This inverts almost every piece of freelancing advice you will read, and it is correct for you specifically because your constraint is time rather than demand.

#### What gets eliminated

| Model | Why it fails at four hours |
| --- | --- |
| Hourly freelancing | You are selling the thing you have least of |
| Marketplace bidding | Hours of unpaid proposal writing per win |
| Many small clients | Overhead swamps the build time |
| Open-ended retainers | "Anything you need" means anything they want |
| Content or audience building | Hundreds of unpaid hours before the first euro |
| Reselling on thin margin | Needs volume, volume needs hours |

*Models that do not survive contact with a four-hour week.*

#### What survives

Three characteristics make a model work at this budget. It should be **front-loaded** — most of the effort happens in a block you can schedule, not spread thinly across every day. It should be **fixed-scope** — the end is defined before you start, so it cannot expand into your school weeks. And it should have a **tail** — something that keeps paying after the work stops.

The catalogue-site product has all three. The build is a block. The scope is a document. The tail is the monthly fee.

---

#### Batching, not trickling

Four hours a week is a bad way to think about your time. Four hours in one Saturday block is worth considerably more than four separate one-hour evenings, because most technical work has a startup cost — remembering where you were, reopening the project, reloading the problem into your head. Call it twenty minutes per session. Four separate sessions lose eighty minutes to startup. One block loses twenty.

Treat the four hours as **one scheduled block plus a small buffer.** Three hours Saturday morning, one hour of email during the week. That is the shape that works.

> **Practical rule**
>
> Never open the project for less than ninety minutes. If you have forty minutes free, use it for email, quoting, or prospect research — the low-startup-cost work — and leave the building for the block.

### 3. The asset you actually own

It is worth being precise about what you are selling, because if you misidentify it you will price it wrong and pitch it wrong.

#### What it is not

It is not HTML and CSS. Plenty of people can write those, including people who will work for less than you. If you pitch yourself as "I make websites" you have entered a crowded, price-competitive market, and you will be compared against Wix, against a template, and against an agency's junior.

It is not design. You are not a designer, and the clients described in Part Five do not want an art piece — they want their catalogue online and findable.

#### What it is

It is a **pipeline for a specific, painful, recurring problem**: a supplier issues a large catalogue as a PDF, and the distributor has no way to get that into a website without typing it all in by hand.

Break down what you actually solved on the Kapriol build:

1. Reading structured product data out of a catalogue PDF across multiple categories — footwear, clothing, protective equipment — each with different field layouts.
2. Normalising that into consistent product JSON that a site can render.
3. Fetching per-product datasheet PDFs in bulk from an endpoint keyed by product ID.
4. Rendering the whole thing as a plain HTML and vanilla JS site that loads fast and needs no server-side platform.

Step four is the commodity part. Steps one through three are the valuable part, and they are the part the client cannot do, cannot hire cheaply, and will have to do again next year when the supplier issues a new catalogue.

> **Say this out loud once**
>
> "I turn supplier catalogues into working product websites, and I keep them updated when the catalogue changes." That is the sentence. It is not "I am a web developer." The first sentence describes a solved problem; the second describes a job title, and job titles get compared on price.

#### Why the recurrence matters more than the build

Catalogues change. Suppliers issue new ranges, discontinue lines, revise prices, add certifications. Every time that happens, the distributor's website is wrong — and a wrong website in a category where product specifications are the whole purchase decision is worse than no website.

That means the client has a permanent problem, not a one-time one. A permanent problem justifies a permanent fee. This is the entire basis of Part Seven, and it is the difference between earning money once and earning money monthly.

#### The compounding you should expect

| Build | Realistic hours | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First (Kapriol) | 25–40 | You were inventing the pipeline |
| Second | 12–18 | Reusing the pipeline, new edge cases |
| Third | 8–12 | Edge cases mostly known |
| Fourth onward | 6–10 | Genuinely a process |

*Hours per build as the pipeline matures. Your price should not fall to match.*

The critical discipline: **your price does not follow your hours down.** The client is buying the outcome, and the outcome is identical whether it took you forty hours or eight. If you drop your price because it got easier, you have handed the entire benefit of your own learning to the customer.

## Part Two — Getting Paid, Legally

> **Read this first**
>
> This part is written from public sources as they stood in mid-2026. Tax and insurance rules in Greece change frequently, sometimes mid-year, and one question in this part — how any of it applies to someone under eighteen — I genuinely cannot answer for you. Treat this as a map of what to ask, not as an answer from an accountant. Take it to whoever handles your family's tax and get it confirmed before you quote anyone a price.

### 4. Why this is the bottleneck

You have the skill, the portfolio piece and a repeatable pipeline. What you do not have is a legal way to accept money from a business. That single gap makes everything else worth zero, because a business cannot pay you without a document that lets them record the expense in their books. Not "will not" — cannot. Their accountant will refuse it.

This is why a €300 cash arrangement is not a shortcut. It is not that it is slightly informal; it is that the client's bookkeeping has no slot for it. A legitimate distributor with an accountant and a VAT number will decline, politely, and you will have wasted the outreach.

So the paperwork is not admin you do later. It is the product's power supply.

#### The two routes

There are two ways a person in Greece receives money for services. One is heavy and one is light, and the light one is almost certainly correct for you.

| | Έναρξη (registering as a freelancer) | Τίτλος κτήσης |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Full self-employed registration | A document for occasional services |
| Who issues the paperwork | You | The client |
| Monthly obligations | Yes, ongoing ΕΦΚΑ contributions | None — deducted per payment |
| Fixed monthly cost | Substantial, owed even at zero income | Zero |
| Annual ceiling | None | Around €10,000 gross |
| Right for you now | No | Yes |

The decisive row is "fixed monthly cost." A registered freelancer in Greece owes insurance contributions every month regardless of whether they earned anything, and those contributions would consume most or all of what a four-hour week can generate. Registering would mean paying for the privilege of a business that is not yet big enough to pay for itself.

### 5. Τίτλος κτήσης, explained properly

The τίτλος κτήσης — formerly called an απόδειξη επαγγελματικής δαπάνης — is the mechanism Greek tax law provides for exactly your situation: a private individual providing a service to a business, occasionally, without having registered at the ΔΟΥ. The ceiling is around €10,000 gross per year.

#### Who does what

This is the part people get backwards, so it is worth being explicit.

1. **You do the work** and agree a gross figure with the client.
2. **The client issues the τίτλος κτήσης.** You do not issue anything. There is no invoice for you to write. Their accountant produces the document.
3. **The client withholds** the insurance contribution and the tax from the gross, pays those over, and pays you the remainder.
4. **You receive the net** and declare the income on your annual return.

The only preparatory step on your side is registration: you need to be registered with ΕΦΚΑ as someone paid via a παραστατικό παρεχόμενων υπηρεσιών, and the client will typically need a signed declaration from you. This is a one-time piece of admin, not a recurring one.

> **What to say to a client**
>
> "I'm paid by τίτλος κτήσης — your accountant issues it, the deductions come off your side, and you pay me the net." Say it early, in the first or second message. It signals you know how this works, which for a young person is worth more than it sounds. Clients relax when the money question has an obvious answer.

#### The deductions

Two amounts come off the gross, in order.

1. **ΕΦΚΑ (insurance).** 13.33% of the gross amount, capped at €155 per document.
2. **Tax.** 20%, calculated on what remains after the ΕΦΚΑ deduction — not on the original gross.

The order matters and is slightly in your favour: because tax is applied after insurance is removed, the effective combined rate is a little below the 33.33% you would get by adding them.

#### Worked figures

| Gross | ΕΦΚΑ | Tax (20% of remainder) | Net to you | Effective rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| €400 | €53.32 | €69.34 | €277.34 | 30.7% |
| €600 | €79.98 | €104.00 | €416.02 | 30.7% |
| €800 | €106.64 | €138.67 | €554.69 | 30.7% |
| €1,000 | €133.30 | €173.34 | €693.36 | 30.7% |
| €1,200 | €155.00 (capped) | €209.00 | €836.00 | 30.3% |
| €1,500 | €155.00 (capped) | €269.00 | €1,076.00 | 28.3% |
| €2,000 | €155.00 (capped) | €369.00 | €1,476.00 | 26.2% |

*Net receipts at common price points. Figures are illustrative; confirm against current rates before quoting.*

Read the last column carefully, because it contains a genuinely useful piece of strategy.

### 6. The cap, and how to bill around it

The insurance contribution is capped at €155 per document. Below roughly €1,163 gross the cap does not bite and you pay a flat 13.33%. Above it, every additional euro carries no further insurance cost at all.

This creates an arbitrage that costs you nothing to exploit: **fewer, larger documents are cheaper than many small ones for the same total.**

#### The demonstration

Suppose you earn €3,000 gross from one client over a year.

| Billing pattern | Documents | Total ΕΦΚΑ | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One annual document of €3,000 | 1 | €155 | — |
| Two of €1,500 | 2 | €310 | €155 worse |
| Four quarterly of €750 | 4 | €400 | €245 worse |
| Twelve monthly of €250 | 12 | €400 | €245 worse |

*The same €3,000 of income, billed four different ways.*

The spread between best and worst here is €245 — which at your prices is a meaningful fraction of an entire build. You capture it by doing nothing except deciding when to bill.

> **The rule this produces**
>
> Bill the monthly fee **annually or semi-annually in advance**, not monthly. Present it to the client as a discount for paying up front, which is true and which they will like. You get the cash sooner, you cross the insurance cap once instead of twelve times, and you eliminate eleven collection conversations a year — which at four hours a week matters as much as the money.

This single decision is worth more than most of the optimisation advice in this document, and it takes one sentence in a proposal to implement. Part Seven gives the exact wording.

#### The tax withholding is not necessarily final

One more thing worth understanding, and worth asking an accountant to confirm for your situation. The 20% deducted is a **withholding** — it is tax collected in advance against your eventual annual liability, not a separate flat charge. When the annual return is filed, that withheld amount is credited against what is actually owed on total income for the year.

If total annual income is low enough to fall under the tax-free threshold, some or all of that withholding may come back. For someone earning a few thousand euros a year, this is not a rounding error. **Do not assume it happens automatically and do not assume it happens at all** — it depends on the whole household's filing situation, which is exactly the sort of thing an accountant answers in five minutes and a document like this cannot.

*Add this to the list of questions in Chapter 7.*

### 7. The question I cannot answer

Everything above describes how τίτλος κτήσης works for an adult. Whether and how it applies to someone under eighteen is a separate question, and it is the one thing standing between you and a functioning business.

I am not going to guess at it. The interaction of minority, tax registration, ΕΦΚΑ registration and the capacity to enter a binding contract is specific, it varies, and getting it wrong is the kind of mistake that lands on your parents rather than on you.

#### The ten-minute phone call

Whoever prepares your family's tax return can answer this. Ask these, in this order, and write down the answers:

1. Can a minor be paid via τίτλος κτήσης in their own name, or does it have to go through a parent?
2. If it must go through a parent — do they already have an ΑΦΜ with a registered activity, or would they also be receiving via τίτλος κτήσης?
3. What do I need to do to register with ΕΦΚΑ as someone paid by παραστατικό παρεχόμενων υπηρεσιών, and can that be done for me now?
4. Does income received this way affect my parents' tax position, tax-free threshold, or any allowance they claim for a dependent?
5. Is the 20% withholding refundable against my annual return at the income levels we are talking about — roughly €1,000 to €4,000 a year?
6. Does a client of mine need anything from me beyond a signed declaration before they can issue the document?

> **Why question 4 matters more than the others**
>
> If your earning creates a tax problem for your parents that is larger than what you earn, the whole plan is worse than not doing it. That is unlikely at these amounts, but it is the sort of thing you find out first rather than in March. Ask it explicitly.

#### If the answer is "it has to go through a parent"

That is a perfectly workable outcome and not a defeat. The client pays into a parent's name against a document their accountant issues; the arrangement between you and your parent is a family matter. It changes nothing about how you find clients, what you charge, or how you deliver. Do not let a paperwork detour become the reason the thing never starts.

#### If the answer is "wait until you are older"

Then two things remain true and worth doing anyway. First, everything in Parts Three through Eight still applies the moment the constraint lifts, and the work you do now on packaging and pipeline does not expire. Second, work done for the family business, or on your own projects, does not need any of this machinery — and a stronger portfolio at sixteen is worth more than a small payment at fourteen.

### 8. Records, from day one

Keep this trivially simple, because at four hours a week any system with friction will be abandoned by March.

One spreadsheet. Six columns. Nothing else.

| Column | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| Date | When you were paid, not when you invoiced |
| Client | Company name |
| Description | "Catalogue site build" or "Annual maintenance 2027" |
| Gross | The agreed figure |
| Net received | What actually arrived |
| Document ref | The τίτλος κτήσης number from their accountant |

Save every τίτλος κτήσης document the client sends you as a PDF, in one folder, named `YYYY-MM-client-amount.pdf`. That is the whole system. It takes ninety seconds per payment and it means that when someone asks you in eighteen months what you earned, you have an answer instead of an archaeology project.

#### The €10,000 line

Watch the running total. The ceiling is roughly €10,000 gross a year, and it is not a cliff you want to discover from the wrong side. Above it, the tax authority may take the view that you are running an actual business and require you to register properly.

At your current time budget you are extremely unlikely to approach this. If you ever do, that is a good problem, and it means the maths has changed enough that registering properly may finally be worth the fixed monthly cost. That is a conversation for an accountant at the time, not a decision to pre-commit to now.

> **Checkpoint**
>
> Do not proceed to Part Five until you can answer, in one sentence, how a client would pay you next month. "My dad's accountant issues a τίτλος κτήσης and I get the net" is an answer. "I'll figure it out when someone says yes" is not, and it is how deals die at the last step.

## Part Three — The Product

### 9. Defining it narrowly

The strongest thing you can do for a business this small is refuse most of the work available to it. A narrow product is faster to build, easier to explain, easier to price and dramatically easier to sell, because a specific promise is believable in a way that "I can build websites" never is.

#### The product, in one sentence

**A fast, static catalogue website built from your supplier's PDF, with every product searchable and filterable, and its own datasheet attached — kept up to date as the catalogue changes.**

Notice what that sentence does not contain. No blog. No online payments. No customer accounts. No content management system. No booking. No newsletter. Each of those is a different product with different failure modes, and each one added to the promise multiplies your build time and your support burden.

#### Why static is a feature, not a limitation

You prefer plain HTML and vanilla JS. In this niche that preference is not a compromise you have to hide — it is genuinely the right architecture, and you should say so with confidence.

| Property | Why the client benefits |
| --- | --- |
| No database | Nothing to hack, nothing to break, nothing to patch monthly |
| No CMS | No login they will forget, no plugin that dies in 2029 |
| Hosting cost near zero | You can host ten clients for pocket change |
| Loads in well under a second | Matters on a phone in a warehouse with poor signal |
| Nothing to maintain | Site works untouched for years |
| Data lives in one JSON file | Regenerating from a new catalogue is a rerun, not a rebuild |

*The static argument, translated from technical terms into client benefits.*

The last row is the commercially important one. Because everything the site displays comes from one structured data file, the annual catalogue update is a pipeline rerun rather than a manual edit of two hundred pages. That is what makes the maintenance fee profitable rather than punishing.

> **How to say it to a client**
>
> Never say "it's a static site." That sounds lesser. Say: "There's no system to log into and nothing to update or patch — it just works, and it loads instantly on a phone. When your catalogue changes, I regenerate it from the new PDF." You have described the same thing as an advantage.

#### The three things every one of these sites must do

Everything else is negotiable; these three are the product.

1. **Every product findable.** Search by name, by code, by category. If a customer knows the article number, they must land on the page in one step.
2. **Every product specified.** Sizes, standards, certifications, materials. In protective equipment the standard number is often the whole purchase decision — a customer buying safety footwear needs to know it meets a specific EN standard, and if your site does not say so, the site is useless to them.
3. **Every product downloadable.** The datasheet PDF attached to the product page. Trade customers forward these to their own clients constantly; it is the single feature distributors appreciate most and it is almost always missing from what they have now.

Get those three right and the site is worth what you charge, even if it is visually plain.

### 10. The pipeline, written down

Write this down properly once. Not for a client — for yourself, in six months, when you have forgotten why you handled the footwear category differently. A documented pipeline is what turns forty hours into eight.

#### The stages

##### Stage one — assess the source

Before quoting anything, spend twenty minutes on the catalogue PDF. You are answering one question: **is the text extractable, or is this a scan?** A born-digital PDF with selectable text is a normal job. A scanned catalogue is a different, much worse job requiring OCR and manual correction, and you either decline it or price it at triple.

Also count: how many products, how many categories, how consistent is the layout between them. A 400-product catalogue with one consistent layout is easier than a 120-product catalogue that changes format every twenty pages.

##### Stage two — extract

Pull structured records out of the PDF. Target a flat list of objects with consistent fields, even where the source is inconsistent. Fields that matter for this niche:

- Article code, exactly as printed, preserving leading zeros
- Product name, in each language the catalogue carries
- Category and subcategory
- Available sizes or variants
- Standards and certifications
- Materials and technical description
- Image reference
- Datasheet identifier, where the supplier exposes one

##### Stage three — enrich

Where the supplier publishes per-product datasheets at a predictable URL keyed by a numeric product ID, fetch them in bulk and store them alongside. This is the step that turns a catalogue listing into something a trade customer will actually use, and it is the step that most impresses a client in a demo.

Be considerate about it: request sequentially with a pause between calls rather than hammering the endpoint. You are not trying to be invisible; you are trying not to be a nuisance to a supplier your client depends on.

##### Stage four — normalise and validate

The step people skip and then regret. Before rendering anything, run checks:

- Every product has a code, and no code appears twice
- Every product has a category that exists in your category list
- Sizes parse into a consistent format
- Every image reference resolves to a file that exists
- Character encoding survived — Greek and any accented characters intact

Write these as a script that prints a list of problems. Run it every time. Ten minutes of validation prevents the client finding four broken products and losing confidence in all six hundred.

##### Stage five — render

The template does the work here, and after the first build the template is the thing you reuse. Category pages, product pages, a search index, a filter panel. Client-side search over a JSON index is entirely adequate at catalogue scale and requires no server.

##### Stage six — the difference pass

For the second and subsequent builds of the same client, do not rebuild — **compare.** Generate the new data, diff it against last year's, and produce a list: products added, removed, changed. That list is what you send the client with the update, and it is the single most valuable thing in the maintenance relationship, because it tells them something about their own catalogue they did not know.

> **The document that makes you money**
>
> "47 products added, 12 discontinued, 31 with revised specifications." A client who receives that once a year understands exactly what they are paying for. Without it, a maintenance fee feels like paying for nothing. Never send an update without the summary.

#### What to keep between projects

| Artefact | Reuse value |
| --- | --- |
| Extraction script skeleton | High — the structure transfers, the field mapping does not |
| Normalisation and validation checks | Very high — almost entirely reusable |
| Bulk datasheet fetcher | Very high — change the URL pattern |
| Site template | Very high — change colours, logo, category names |
| Search and filter code | Total — it does not care what the products are |
| Deployment steps | Total — write them once as a checklist |

*Where the compounding comes from. Only the first row genuinely needs redoing per client.*

### 11. What to charge

Pricing is the highest-leverage hour in this entire document. An hour spent deciding to charge €800 instead of €400 is worth more than twenty hours of building, and it costs nothing.

#### The three tiers

Offer three. Not one, not five. Three, with the middle one designed to be chosen.

| | **Basic** | **Standard** | **Full** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price | €450 | €800 | €1,400 |
| Products | up to 150 | up to 600 | unlimited |
| Categories and search | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Product filters | Basic | Full | Full |
| Datasheet downloads | No | Yes | Yes |
| Languages | One | Two | Two |
| Contact form | No | Yes | Yes |
| Enquiry basket | No | No | Yes |
| Revision rounds | One | Two | Two |
| Delivery | 3 weeks | 4 weeks | 6 weeks |

*Adjust the numbers to your market, but keep the shape: three tiers, a clear middle, and a top tier that makes the middle look reasonable.*

#### Why three tiers works

The top tier is not really there to be sold. It is there to make the middle tier look sensible, and to catch the occasional client who has more budget than you assumed. Without it, your €800 option is "the expensive one." With it, €800 is "the reasonable one." This is a well-documented effect and it costs you one extra column in a table.

The bottom tier is there so that price-sensitive clients have somewhere to go that is not "no." Make it genuinely less good — fewer products, no datasheets, one revision — so that choosing it is a real trade-off rather than a bargain.

> **The line that decides the sale**
>
> Always present the monthly maintenance fee inside the same table, in the same conversation. **"€800 to build, then €50 a month to keep it current — or €500 for the year if you prefer to settle it annually."** Said at quoting time, this is a normal part of the offer. Said three months later, it is an unwelcome surprise and roughly half of clients will decline.

#### Deliberately not offered

Say no to these, every time, with a friendly reason:

- **Online payments.** A different product with different liability, and trade distributors mostly sell on account anyway.
- **A CMS or admin login.** The moment they can edit it, every problem becomes your problem to diagnose.
- **Logo and brand design.** Not your skill and it will consume more hours than the build.
- **Ongoing SEO.** Open-ended by nature, unmeasurable at this scale, unbounded in hours.
- **Anything urgent.** You have four hours a week. A rush job breaks the whole system for a small premium.

#### Reading the client's budget

You will get better at this, but three quick signals:

| Signal | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| Asks the price in the first message | Price-sensitive; lead with Basic |
| Asks how long it will take | Deadline-driven; can pay for certainty |
| Mentions a competitor's site | Comparison-driven; show Standard and Full |
| Multiple suppliers, several catalogues | Bigger operation; start at Standard |
| "We just need something simple" | Almost always means Basic, and that is fine |

*Early signals worth reading before you quote.*

#### The discount you are allowed to give

One, and only one: **a discount for paying the annual maintenance up front.** Twelve months for the price of ten. It costs you nothing you were not going to do anyway, it gets cash in early, and as Chapter 6 showed, it is cheaper for you in insurance contributions than billing monthly.

Never discount the build price to win a client. A client won on price will negotiate every subsequent thing, and at four hours a week you cannot afford a customer who costs you hours arguing.

## Part Four — The Build, in Detail

*This part is the technical core. If you are reading straight through for strategy, skip to Part Five and come back when you have a signed scope agreement.*

### 12. Extraction strategy

Everything downstream depends on getting clean records out of the PDF. Time invested here returns tenfold; time saved here costs twentyfold in manual correction later.

#### Establish the shape before writing anything

Open the catalogue and answer four questions in writing before any code:

1. **How many distinct page layouts are there?** Most supplier catalogues have three to six: a category divider, a grid of products, a detailed single-product page, a size or standards table. Each layout needs its own handling.
2. **What identifies a product?** Usually an article code. Note its exact format, including whether it has leading zeros, prefixes per category, or size suffixes appended.
3. **Where do the specifications live?** Sometimes inline with each product, sometimes in a shared table at the end of a section covering forty products at once. The second case is far more common than people expect and is the main reason naive extraction produces products with no specifications.
4. **What is the language situation?** Bilingual catalogues often interleave languages in the same text block, which will produce garbage unless you handle it deliberately.

> **The twenty-minute rule**
>
> Spend twenty minutes on these four questions before quoting, not after. A catalogue where specifications live in shared tables is roughly double the work of one where they sit with each product, and you want to know that before you name a price.

#### The pass structure

Do not try to extract everything in one sweep. Three passes, each simple, beats one pass that handles every case.

**Pass one — segment.** Walk the pages and classify each into a layout type. Output: a list of page ranges by category and layout. Verify this by hand against the actual PDF; it takes five minutes and catches the errors that would otherwise propagate through everything.

**Pass two — harvest.** For each page, pull raw candidate records. Do not clean, do not normalise, do not discard anything that looks wrong. Keep the page number on every record. Output: a large, messy list.

**Pass three — resolve.** Now merge: attach shared specification tables to the products they describe, split interleaved languages, normalise codes, and drop the rows that were headers or footnotes. Output: clean records.

Keeping these separate means that when something is wrong you can tell which pass caused it, and you can rerun one pass instead of all of them.

#### The field mapping document

For each client, keep a short file recording how their catalogue maps to your data model. It is the only genuinely client-specific artefact in the pipeline and it is what makes next year's update take two hours.

~~~text
KAPRIOL — field map, 2026 edition

Pages 1–4       front matter, skip
Pages 5–48      footwear      layout: grid + shared spec table p.47
Pages 49–92     clothing      layout: grid, specs inline
Pages 93–128    PPE           layout: single-product pages

Article code    format: 4 digits, leading zeros significant
                appears: top-left of each product block
Sizes           footwear: EU numeric range in shared table
                clothing: S–XXXL, inline
Standards       "EN ISO xxxxx" strings, inline, may be multiple
Datasheet       PrintPdf endpoint, numeric productid ≈ 4 digits
Images          referenced by article code, .jpg

Known issues    p.63 two products share one image reference
                p.71 size table spans a page break
~~~

The "known issues" section is the most valuable part. Next year you will not remember any of it.

### 13. The data model

Keep it flat and boring. Every hour spent on a clever schema is an hour not spent selling.

~~~text
{
  "code":        "0342",
  "name":        { "el": "...", "en": "..." },
  "category":    "footwear",
  "subcategory": "safety-boots",
  "description": { "el": "...", "en": "..." },
  "sizes":       ["38","39","40","41","42","43","44","45","46"],
  "standards":   ["EN ISO 20345", "S3", "SRC"],
  "materials":   "...",
  "image":       "img/0342.jpg",
  "datasheet":   "pdf/0342.pdf",
  "supplier":    "kapriol",
  "edition":     "2026"
}
~~~

#### Why these fields specifically

**`code` as a string, always.** The moment it becomes a number, leading zeros vanish and article 0342 becomes 342, which matches nothing in the client's own system. This is the single most common data bug in catalogue work.

**`edition`.** Records which catalogue version a product came from. This is what makes the annual diff possible, and the diff is what justifies the maintenance fee.

**Language as a nested object,** not separate product records. One product, multiple labels. Separate records per language doubles your data and guarantees the two drift apart.

**`standards` as an array.** In protective equipment a product commonly meets several. Storing them as one string makes filtering by standard impossible, and filtering by standard is the feature trade customers actually use.

#### What not to put in the model

Prices. Distributors price differently per customer, prices change constantly, and publishing them turns a catalogue into a shop with all the obligations that entails. If the client insists, put prices in a separate file so the product data stays stable — but push back first. Most trade distributors do not want public pricing and have simply never been asked.

### 14. Search that actually works

Search is the feature clients demo to their own staff, so it is worth the extra hour.

#### The requirements

| Requirement | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Match on article code, exactly | The commonest lookup by far |
| Match on partial code | People remember four of six digits |
| Match on name, in both languages | The second commonest lookup |
| Tolerate Greek accents and case | "ΜΠΟΤΑ" must find "μπότα" |
| Tolerate Greek and Latin lookalikes | Users type "MПOTA" with mixed alphabets |
| Return in under 50ms | It should feel instant |

*The fifth row is specific to Greek and is skipped by almost everyone. Handling it is a small piece of code and a noticeable difference.*

#### The approach

Build a search index at generation time, not at page load. For each product, produce a normalised search string: the code, the name in each language lowercased and stripped of accents, the category, and the standards, all concatenated. Ship that as part of the JSON.

At query time, normalise the user's input the same way and do a substring match across the index. At catalogue scale — hundreds to a few thousand products — this is fast enough that optimising further is a waste of your time.

> **Normalisation is the whole trick**
>
> Apply the same transformation to the index and the query: lowercase, strip diacritics, map visually identical Latin and Greek characters to one form, collapse whitespace. Do it once in a single function used by both sides. Every search bug you will ever have comes from the index and the query being normalised differently.

#### Ranking, briefly

Do not build a relevance engine. A three-tier sort is enough and takes ten minutes: exact code matches first, then matches that start with the query, then matches that contain it anywhere. Within each tier, sort alphabetically. Nobody has ever complained about this.

### 15. Filters

Filters are what make a catalogue browsable rather than merely searchable, and they are where the specification data pays off.

#### Which filters to build

Derive them from the data rather than deciding in advance. After extraction, count the distinct values of every field. Any field with between three and about twenty distinct values, present on most products, is a good filter. Fields with two values are better as a toggle; fields with hundreds are not filters at all.

For protective equipment this typically produces: category, subcategory, size, standard, colour, and material.

#### The rules that make filters feel right

- **Show counts next to each option.** "S3 (47)" tells the user whether it is worth clicking.
- **Disable options that would return nothing,** rather than hiding them. Hiding makes the interface jump around; disabling teaches the user the shape of the catalogue.
- **Multiple selections within one filter are OR, across filters are AND.** This is what everyone expects, and doing it the other way produces a filter that always returns nothing.
- **Put the state in the URL.** A filtered view the client can send to a customer as a link is worth disproportionately more than the effort it costs.
- **One reset button,** always visible once any filter is active.

The URL point deserves emphasis. Trade distributors constantly send customers links to "the S3 boots in size 43." If your filters produce a shareable URL, you have built them a sales tool rather than a browsing interface, and they will notice.

### 16. Performance and the mobile case

Your buyers are frequently on a phone, on mobile data, in a warehouse or on a site with poor signal. This is not a hypothetical — it is the normal usage condition in this sector, and it is why the static architecture is genuinely correct rather than merely convenient.

#### The budget

| Metric | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Initial HTML | under 20 KB |
| Product JSON, compressed | under 200 KB for 600 products |
| Images | lazy-loaded, correctly sized, modern format |
| Total for a first view | under 400 KB |
| Time to usable on a slow connection | under 2 seconds |
| JavaScript dependencies | zero |

*These are achievable with plain HTML and vanilla JS and essentially unachievable with a typical framework build.*

#### Where the weight actually goes

It is always images. Text and JSON are negligible; a single unoptimised product photograph from a supplier can exceed the entire rest of the page by an order of magnitude. Resize every image to the largest size actually displayed, convert to a modern format, and lazy-load anything below the fold. That one step is most of the performance work.

> **The demo that closes deals**
>
> Open the client's current site and yours side by side on a phone, on mobile data, in front of them. Do not explain the architecture. Let them watch one load instantly and the other not. This single demonstration is more persuasive than every paragraph in this chapter.

### 17. Deployment and handover

#### Hosting

Static hosting is close to free and there are several good options. Choose one and use it for every client — the consistency is worth more than any feature difference. Your criteria: it serves static files over HTTPS, it handles custom domains, and it will still exist in five years.

Keep the client's domain registration in the **client's** name and account wherever possible. You manage the DNS records; they own the domain. This matters enormously if the relationship ever ends: a client who cannot get their own domain back will tell people about it, and rightly.

#### The handover note

One page, sent when the site goes live. It costs fifteen minutes and prevents most support email forever.


#### Keep your own copy

Archive, per client: the source catalogue PDF, the generated JSON, the field map, and the deployed site. Storage is free and next year's update depends on all four. Losing the field map turns a two-hour update into a rebuild.

## Part Five — Finding Clients

### 18. The target profile

At four hours a week you cannot afford to talk to the wrong people. Every unqualified conversation costs twenty minutes you do not have, and the failure mode of outreach is not rejection — it is a polite, extended, ultimately pointless correspondence.

So define the target tightly enough that you can disqualify someone in thirty seconds.

#### The profile

**A distributor or importer of catalogue products, with between two and forty employees, whose supplier issues a large annual PDF catalogue, and whose current website either does not exist, does not list products, or lists them badly.**

Break that into checkable criteria:

| Criterion | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Distributes rather than manufactures | Manufacturers own their data; distributors receive it as a PDF |
| 2–40 employees | Big enough to afford it, small enough to decide fast |
| Supplier issues a PDF catalogue | This is the actual problem you solve |
| Products with specifications | Standards, sizes, certifications — makes a catalogue site necessary |
| Website absent or product-free | The gap you fill |
| Sells to trade, not consumers | No need for payments, baskets or accounts |

*Six criteria. A prospect failing two or more is not worth an email.*

#### Why this profile and not another

The instinct is to target restaurants, cafés, small shops — they are everywhere and they all need websites. Resist it. Those businesses have no catalogue problem, so you would be selling a generic website into a market saturated with cheap templates, competing on price against Wix. Your advantage evaporates entirely.

The catalogue distributor is different in three ways that matter. Their problem is **specific**, so your pitch can be specific. Their problem is **recurring**, which justifies the maintenance fee. And their problem is **technically annoying**, which means very few people are offering to solve it, so you are not in a price fight.

> **The test for any niche**
>
> "Does this client have a problem that comes back every year, that is genuinely fiddly, and that almost nobody local is offering to solve?" Catalogue distributors pass all three. Restaurants pass none.

#### Adjacent niches with the same shape

Once the pipeline is proven, the same product transfers with almost no change to any sector where a supplier pushes a PDF catalogue down to a distributor:

- Safety and protective equipment — where you already have a portfolio piece
- Workwear and industrial clothing
- Hand and power tools
- Industrial fasteners, fixings and abrasives
- Electrical components and installation materials
- Plumbing, heating and sanitary ware
- Laboratory and medical consumables
- Automotive and agricultural parts
- Packaging materials
- Hospitality and catering equipment

Do not spread across these. Pick the one where you already have a reference — protective equipment and workwear — and stay there until you have sold three. A client is dramatically more likely to buy when your example site is from their own sector, because it removes the imaginative leap.

### 19. Where they actually are

You need a list. Not a big one — thirty to fifty qualified names is enough for a year at your pace.

#### Sources, in order of usefulness

1. **Supplier dealer locators.** The single best source, and almost nobody uses it. Manufacturers of workwear and protective equipment publish lists of their authorised distributors by country, because they want customers to find them. That page is a pre-qualified prospect list: every company on it, by definition, receives a PDF catalogue from that supplier. Work through the manufacturers whose products your target market carries.
2. **Trade fair exhibitor lists.** Safety, construction and industrial trade fairs publish exhibitor directories, and they stay online for years. Every exhibitor is a company that spends money on being visible.
3. **Trade association member directories.** Sector associations list members with contact details.
4. **B2B directories and the business pages.** Lower quality, higher volume. Useful for filling gaps.
5. **Skroutz itself.** You already know this platform well. Merchants selling workwear and protective equipment there are distributors with a catalogue problem, and you can see which ones have thin or missing product information. This is an advantage specific to you that a stranger to the platform does not have.

> **The best twenty minutes of prospecting you can do**
>
> Open the dealer locator of a large protective-equipment manufacturer, filter to Greece and neighbouring countries, and open every listed distributor's website in a tab. Within twenty minutes you will have a shortlist of companies whose sites do not list products at all. Those are your prospects, and they are pre-qualified in a way no directory can match.

#### The list

One spreadsheet, one row per company, seven columns:

| Column | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| Company | Name |
| Country and city | Geography matters for language and payment |
| Products | Which sector, which supplier brands |
| Site status | None / brochure only / products but bad / good |
| Contact | Email, and a name if you can find one |
| Contacted | Date, or blank |
| Outcome | No reply / declined / interested / won |

Add to it in fifteen-minute gaps. Prospecting is low-startup-cost work — exactly the sort of thing that fits in the awkward half hours where building would be wasteful.

### 20. Qualifying before you write

Two minutes per prospect, before any email. You are looking for evidence that the problem you solve exists at this specific company.

#### The two-minute check

1. Does the site list products? If it does, and it does so well, disqualify — the problem is solved.
2. Can you find the supplier catalogue PDF? Often it is linked from their own site, or the supplier's. If you can find it, you can quote accurately, and you can reference it specifically in the email.
3. Is there a real person to write to? A named contact or a role address beats a generic one substantially.
4. Does the site look abandoned? Copyright year long past, broken links, a "coming soon" page. Abandonment is a strong buying signal — someone once cared enough to start.
5. Are they on Skroutz or similar with thin product data? Direct evidence they have data problems.

#### The disqualifiers

Walk away, without an email, if any of these are true:

- The site already has a good, searchable product catalogue
- The company is large enough to have a marketing department
- Everything they sell is a handful of products with no specifications
- They are a manufacturer with their own product data systems
- There is no way to contact a human

> **A note on rejection**
>
> Most of your emails will get no reply. That is the normal, expected outcome and it is not about you. A five to ten percent reply rate on well-targeted, specific outreach is good. Thirty emails to well-qualified prospects should produce two or three real conversations and, realistically, one client. One client is what you need.

### 21. The outreach email

Almost all cold outreach fails for the same reason: it is about the sender. Yours will be about the recipient's specific catalogue, which is why it will do better than most.

#### The rules

- **Under 120 words.** It is read on a phone between other things.
- **Name their actual catalogue in the first line.** This is the entire trick. Generic outreach gets deleted; "your Kapriol footwear range" gets read.
- **One specific observation** about their current site. Not an insult — an observation.
- **No attachments.** They trip spam filters and nobody opens them.
- **One link,** to a live example.
- **Ask for a reply, not a meeting.** A meeting is a big ask; "worth a look?" is a small one.
- **No mention of your age.** Not hiding it — it is simply not relevant to whether they need a catalogue site, and volunteering it invites a decision on the wrong basis. Chapter 22 covers what to do when it comes up.

### 22. When the age question comes up

It will come up, usually late, usually as surprise rather than objection. Handle it in one short paragraph and move immediately back to the work.

#### The principles

**Do not lead with it.** Not because it is shameful, but because it is not decision-relevant, and volunteering it invites the client to evaluate you on it rather than on the work.

**Do not hide it.** If asked directly, answer directly. Getting caught being evasive about something small destroys trust about everything large.

**Answer, then redirect within the same breath.** The redirect is what matters. Every second spent discussing your age is a second not spent discussing their catalogue.

#### What to say

~~~text
Yes — I'm still at school, which is why I take a small number of
projects and why the scope and timeline are written down clearly
before I start. The invoicing is handled properly through
[τίτλος κτήσης / my parent's business], so your accountant has
everything they need.

The example at [LINK] is a live site I built. Happy to walk you
through how it handles your catalogue.
~~~

That is the entire answer. Three things: acknowledged, addressed the two real concerns underneath it — will this get finished, and can I pay you legally — and returned to the work.

#### The two objections that actually hide behind it

Nobody's real worry is your birth year. Their real worries are:

1. **"Will this be finished?"** Answered by a written scope, a delivery date, and a live example of something already finished. Your Kapriol site does more work here than any reassurance you could write.
2. **"Can I pay you in a way my accountant accepts?"** Answered by having Part Two sorted. This is precisely why the paperwork is chapter four and not chapter twenty.

Address those two and the age question dissolves, because it was never really the question.

> **The clients who care least**
>
> Small distributors run by people who started young themselves are your best market, and they are common in this sector. Many of them built their business from a van. Competence at fourteen reads to them as familiar rather than alarming — and a working example site is competence they can click on.

## Part Six — Closing and Scoping

### 23. The proposal

A proposal does three jobs: it names a price, it defines the boundary of the work, and it makes the client feel the thing is already real. One page. Not three.

Send it as a PDF, not in the body of an email. A PDF is a document; an email is a conversation, and conversations get negotiated.

#### The structure

~~~text
CATALOGUE WEBSITE — [COMPANY NAME]
Proposal, [DATE]. Valid 30 days.

WHAT YOU GET
A website listing your complete [BRAND] catalogue, built from the
supplier PDF, with:
  · Search by article code or product name
  · Filters by category, size and standard
  · A page per product with full specifications
  · The technical datasheet downloadable on every product page
  · Works on phones; loads in under a second
  · Greek and English

WHAT IT COSTS
Build ................................................ €800
Maintenance, per year, billed annually ............... €500
  (covers catalogue updates when your supplier issues a new
   edition, hosting, and small corrections)

TIMELINE
Start ....................... within 1 week of deposit
Draft for review ............ week 3
Live ........................ week 4

HOW IT WORKS
  · €300 deposit to start, €500 on delivery
  · Two rounds of revisions included
  · Payment by τίτλος κτήσης — your accountant issues the
    document, deductions are handled on your side

WHAT I NEED FROM YOU
  · The current catalogue PDF
  · Your logo, if you have a digital file
  · Contact details for the site footer

NOT INCLUDED
Online payments, customer accounts, logo design, ongoing SEO.
Available separately if needed, quoted at the time.

[NAME] · [EMAIL] · [PHONE]
~~~

#### Why each part is there

**"Valid 30 days"** creates a soft deadline without pressure. It also lets you raise your price later without awkwardness.

**The maintenance line sits inside the price block,** not in a footnote and not in a later conversation. This is the single most important formatting decision in the document. A client reading the price block sees both numbers as one offer and accepts or rejects them together. Separate them and you are making the sale twice, and the second one is much harder.

**The deposit** is not primarily about money. It is a filter. A client who will not pay a deposit is a client who was not going to pay at all, and finding that out before you spend fifteen hours is worth far more than the €300.

**"Two rounds of revisions"** is your defence against the single largest threat to a four-hour week, which is scope creep. It must be in writing before you start, because after you start it sounds like a complaint.

**"Not included"** looks negative and does the opposite. It tells the client you have thought about the boundary, which reads as professionalism, and it means the conversation about extras happens on your terms.

> **The commonest mistake**
>
> Quoting a price in an email, casually, in a sentence. "It'd be around 700 or so." That is not a proposal, it is an opening bid, and you have just invited a negotiation in which you have no document to point back to. Always send the PDF.

### 24. The scope agreement

For a build at these prices you do not need a lawyer's contract. You need one page that both of you have agreed to, in writing, before work starts. Its purpose is not to win a court case. Its purpose is to be the thing you can point at, kindly, when someone asks for the eleventh change.

~~~text
SCOPE AGREEMENT
[COMPANY] and [YOUR NAME] — [DATE]

1. THE WORK
   A static catalogue website for [COMPANY] containing the
   products in [CATALOGUE NAME, EDITION], approximately [N]
   products across [N] categories.
   Features: search, category and attribute filters, product
   pages with specifications, datasheet downloads, contact form.
   Languages: [Greek, English].

2. NOT THE WORK
   Anything not listed in section 1. In particular: online
   payment, customer logins, blog, newsletter, logo or brand
   design, ongoing SEO work, product photography, writing
   product descriptions from scratch, and translation beyond
   what the supplier catalogue already contains.

3. WHAT [COMPANY] PROVIDES
   The catalogue PDF, a logo file, contact details, and one
   named person who gives feedback and approves. Feedback from
   one person only.

4. REVISIONS
   Two rounds of revisions after the draft is delivered.
   A round means one consolidated list of changes.
   Further rounds: €[X] each, agreed before starting.

5. TIMELINE
   Draft by [DATE]. Live by [DATE].
   Dates move by however long feedback takes beyond one week.

6. PRICE AND PAYMENT
   €[X] total. €[Y] deposit before work begins, remainder on
   delivery. Paid by τίτλος κτήσης.

7. MAINTENANCE
   €[X] per year from launch, billed annually in advance.
   Covers: one full catalogue update per year when the supplier
   issues a new edition, hosting, and small text or contact
   corrections.
   Does not cover: new features, new sections, redesign.
   Either side may end it with 30 days' notice.

8. WHAT HAPPENS TO THE FILES
   On final payment, [COMPANY] owns the site content and data.
   The build scripts and templates remain mine and are reused
   across projects.

9. IF IT STOPS
   If [COMPANY] pauses the project for more than 60 days, the
   deposit is not refunded and restarting is requoted.

Agreed:
[COMPANY] ______________________   Date ____________
[YOUR NAME] ____________________   Date ____________
~~~

#### The clauses that earn their place

**Section 3, "feedback from one person only."** Underrated and enormously protective. Three people giving contradictory feedback is how a four-week project becomes a four-month project, and it is nobody's fault, which makes it impossible to push back on without this clause.

**Section 4, "a round means one consolidated list."** Without this, "two rounds" becomes twenty emails each containing one change, and you have spent your revision allowance on the delivery mechanism rather than the changes.

**Section 5, "dates move by however long feedback takes."** Clients are slow. That is normal and not malicious. This clause means their slowness does not become your emergency.

**Section 8, on the scripts.** This is the clause that protects the business. The client owns their site and their data — entirely fair, they paid for it. You keep the pipeline, because the pipeline is the asset that makes the next client profitable. Nobody has ever objected to this.

**Section 9, on pausing.** Projects stall. The client gets busy, the person who cared leaves, a supplier is late. This clause means a stalled project does not sit in your head, unpaid and unfinished, for six months.

> **On enforcement**
>
> You are not going to sue anyone over €800, and everyone involved knows it. That is not what this document is for. It is for the moment three weeks in when someone says "and could it also have a login area?" and you can reply, warmly, "that's outside what we scoped — want me to price it?" Without the page, that reply sounds like reluctance. With it, it sounds like process.

### 25. The deposit, and getting paid

#### Never start without the deposit

Not as a matter of trust. As a matter of your own time budget. Fifteen unpaid hours is nearly four weeks of your entire available capacity, and you cannot get those weeks back.

The deposit also changes the client's behaviour, immediately and measurably. A client who has paid something responds to emails, sends the logo file, and turns up to look at the draft. A client who has paid nothing does none of those things, and their inattention is what turns a four-week project into a four-month one.

#### The sequence

| Stage | What happens | Trigger for the next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agreement signed | Scope agreed in writing | — |
| Deposit paid | Roughly 40% of total | Work begins |
| Draft delivered | Live on a temporary address | Feedback within 7 days |
| Revisions | Two rounds, consolidated | Approval |
| Balance paid | Remaining 60% | — |
| Site goes live | Moved to their domain | — |
| Maintenance begins | Annual fee, billed now | — |

*Note the order of the last three rows.*

#### The most important row in that table

**The balance is paid before the site goes live on their domain.** Not after. This is not distrust — it is simply how the industry works, and stating it plainly at proposal stage means it never becomes awkward.

The draft lives at a temporary address they can see, click and test. That is enough for them to be confident. The move to their own domain is the last step, and it happens after payment. Do this and you will essentially never have a collection problem. Do it the other way and you will eventually spend eleven emails over five months chasing €500, which at your hourly value is a catastrophe.

> **The one sentence that prevents this entire problem**
>
> In the proposal: "Site goes live on your domain once the balance is settled." Said once, at the start, in writing, it is unremarkable. Said at the end, for the first time, it sounds like a hostage situation.

#### If someone does not pay

Two emails, one call, then stop. Do not escalate, do not spend your Saturday block on it, do not let it occupy your head. The site is not on their domain, so you have lost the build hours but nothing else, and the lesson is worth the tuition.

Write down what you would do differently. Almost always the answer is: they hesitated over the deposit and I went ahead anyway.

## Part Seven — The Retainer

### 26. Why this is the whole business

A build pays you once. You do fifteen hours of work, you receive €800, and then you are back where you started, needing another client. That is a treadmill, and treadmills do not work at four hours a week because the running never stops.

The maintenance fee is different in kind. It pays while you are at school. It pays in exam week. It pays in August. Ten clients at €500 a year is €5,000 arriving whether or not you opened a laptop, against maybe fifteen hours of actual work across the whole year.

That is the business. The builds are how you acquire the annuity.

#### The arithmetic over three years

| | Builds only | Builds plus maintenance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year 1: 2 clients | €1,600 | €1,600 + €1,000 |
| Year 2: 2 more | €1,600 | €1,600 + €2,000 |
| Year 3: 2 more | €1,600 | €1,600 + €3,000 |
| Three-year total | €4,800 | €10,800 |
| Hours in year 3 | Same as year 1 | Slightly more |

*Same number of clients, same effort, more than double the money. The gap is entirely the maintenance fee.*

Look at the last row. The maintenance income more than doubles the total while adding only a few hours a year, because a catalogue update is a pipeline rerun. This is the closest thing to free money that exists in a services business, and the only reason people do not have it is that they forget to ask for it at the right moment.

#### Why it must be quoted at the start

There is a narrow window in which the maintenance fee is easy to sell, and it is the moment the client is already deciding to spend money. Inside that window, €500 a year attached to an €800 build is a detail. Outside it, three months later, €500 a year for a site that is currently working fine is a new purchase requiring a new decision, and roughly half of clients will say no.

> **The rule, stated once more because it is the most valuable sentence in this document**
>
> The maintenance fee goes in the price block of the proposal, on the line directly beneath the build price. Never in a footnote, never "we can discuss that later," never in a separate email.

### 27. Pitching it so it sounds obvious

The fee must sound like a consequence of how the thing works, not like an upsell. The framing that does this is **the catalogue changes.**

#### The pitch

~~~text
The one thing to know about a catalogue site is that it's only as
current as the catalogue. When [SUPPLIER] issues a new edition —
usually once a year — the site needs regenerating or it starts
telling your customers about products you no longer sell.

That's what the annual fee covers: I rebuild it from the new PDF,
send you a summary of what changed, and handle hosting and any
small corrections through the year. €500 a year, billed annually.

If you'd rather handle it yourself later, that's fine too — the
site keeps working, it just gets gradually out of date.
~~~

The last paragraph is doing quiet, important work. Offering the client a genuine way out removes the pressure, which makes the offer feel like information rather than a sales tactic. Almost nobody takes the way out, because "gradually out of date" is exactly the outcome they were trying to escape by hiring you.

#### What the fee actually covers

Be specific. Vagueness here is what turns a retainer into unlimited free work.

| Included | Not included |
| --- | --- |
| One full catalogue update per year | A second update mid-year |
| Hosting and domain renewal handling | A new domain |
| Small text corrections | New pages or sections |
| Fixing anything broken | New features |
| Contact detail changes | Redesign |
| Adding or removing a few products | Adding a new supplier's catalogue |

*Print this table into the scope agreement. It is the boundary that makes the fee profitable.*

The right-hand column is not a refusal. It is a price list. "That's outside the maintenance, but I can quote it" is a sentence that generates additional revenue rather than resentment, and clients who have the boundary explained up front use it comfortably.

#### The annual update, in practice

Roughly two hours, once a year:

1. Get the new catalogue PDF, or notice it published (30 min, mostly waiting)
2. Rerun the extraction pipeline (20 min)
3. Run the validation checks and fix what they flag (30 min)
4. Diff against last year, generate the change summary (15 min)
5. Deploy (10 min)
6. Send the client the summary (15 min)

Two hours for €500 is an extraordinary rate, and it is entirely legitimate: you are being paid for having built the pipeline, not for the two hours.

> **Never skip step 6**
>
> The change summary is the product. Without it the client experiences the maintenance fee as money leaving their account for no visible reason, and that is the client who cancels in year three. With it, they receive a document telling them something useful about their own catalogue. That document is what renews the fee.

## Part Eight — Defending the Four Hours

### 28. Scope creep, precisely

Scope creep is not clients being difficult. It is a structural property of building something someone can see: as it becomes real, they think of more things it could do. Every one of those thoughts is reasonable in isolation. Collectively they destroy a small time budget.

At four hours a week, an extra ninety minutes is nearly half a week. Three unbudgeted requests is a fortnight. This is why the defence has to be systematic rather than a matter of willpower in the moment.

#### The three forms

**The addition.** "Could it also have X?" The easiest to handle, because it is visibly new.

**The refinement.** "Could the search also match partial codes?" Harder, because it sounds like a correction rather than an addition. Ask whether it was in the scope document. If it was not, it is an addition wearing a disguise.

**The drift.** No single request, just a slow accumulation of small things across many messages. The most dangerous kind, because there is never a moment at which it is natural to object. Defended against only by the consolidated-list rule.

### 29. Twelve requests and what to say

These are the ones that actually arrive. Having the reply already written means you answer in ninety seconds instead of drafting for twenty minutes and agreeing out of awkwardness.

##### "Could we also add a shop / online payments?"
~~~text
That's a bigger piece — payments bring in checkout, delivery and
returns handling. Outside this build, but I can price it as a
separate project once this one is live.
~~~

##### "Can I edit the products myself?"
~~~text
Deliberately not, and it's the reason the site is fast and never
breaks. The products come straight from the supplier catalogue —
when it changes, I regenerate it. That's what the annual update
covers.
~~~

##### "Can you also do our logo?"
~~~text
Not my area, I'd do a worse job than someone who does it properly.
Send me whatever file you have and I'll work with it.
~~~

##### "My colleague has some thoughts too."
~~~text
Happy to take them — could you collect everyone's comments into
one list and send it as a single message? Feedback from several
directions at once is how projects get slow.
~~~

##### "Can we see it in a different colour?"
~~~text
Sure, that's within the revision rounds. Send me any other changes
at the same time so we use one round rather than two.
~~~

##### "Could you add our second supplier's catalogue?"
~~~text
Yes — that's a second catalogue, so it's priced separately. It's
cheaper than the first because the site already exists: around
€[X]. Want me to send a quote?
~~~

##### "We need it two weeks earlier."
~~~text
I can't compress this one — I work on a fixed schedule around
school. If the date is critical I'd rather tell you now than
promise and miss it. The delivery date in the agreement is one
I'll hit.
~~~

##### "Can you make it rank first on Google?"
~~~text
No one can promise that. What I can do is make sure the site is
technically sound for search — fast, structured properly, every
product on its own page with real content. That's most of what's
within anyone's control.
~~~

##### "Could you just quickly change…"
~~~text
Of course. Adding it to the list for this revision round.
~~~

*"Just quickly" is the phrase that precedes most scope creep. Accept it, but log it against a round — that is what makes the round countable.*

##### "Can you look at our other site while you're at it?"
~~~text
Happy to take a look and tell you what I'd do — that part's free.
If it turns into work, I'd quote it separately.
~~~

##### "What if we don't like it?"
~~~text
That's what the two revision rounds are for — we shape it until
it's right. The draft goes up somewhere you can click around
before anything goes live on your domain.
~~~

##### "Can you take a call to discuss?"
~~~text
Email works better for me and it means everything's written down.
If something genuinely needs a call I can do [DAY] evening.
~~~

> **The tone that makes all of these work**
>
> Warm, immediate, unapologetic. Not "I'm so sorry but unfortunately" — that reads as reluctance and invites a push. Just the fact, then the alternative. Every one of these replies says yes to something, which is why none of them feel like a refusal.

### 30. Communication rules

#### One weekly window

Answer client email once a week, at a fixed time. Say so in the first message: "I check messages on [day] — if something's urgent, phone me."

This sounds unprofessional and is the opposite. It sets an expectation you can meet reliably every single week for years, which is far more professional than replying within an hour for three weeks and then vanishing during exams. Clients do not need fast; they need predictable.

#### Never let a question sit unanswered

An unanswered email costs you more than the answer would. It sits in your head, the client sends a chasing message, and you now have two emails to deal with instead of one. Even "got it, looking at this on Saturday" closes the loop and costs eight seconds.

#### Write decisions down

After any call, send a short summary: "To confirm — we agreed X, Y and Z, and I'll have the draft by [date]." Two minutes, and it eliminates the entire category of disagreement in which two people remember a conversation differently.

#### The weekly shape

| When | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saturday, 3 hours | The build block | Long enough to get past startup cost |
| One weekday, 30 min | Email window | Predictable, batched, closes loops |
| Odd 15-min gaps | Prospecting, list-building | Low startup cost, fits anywhere |
| Never | Anything urgent | The system has no slack for it |

*Four hours, allocated. The last row is a commitment, not an aspiration.*

#### Protecting the block

The Saturday block is the business. Everything else is administration around it. Defend it the way you would defend an exam: it is in the calendar, it does not move for anything short of a genuine emergency, and it is not the time you use for email.

If the block gets eaten three weeks running, you do not have a four-hour week — you have a zero-hour week with correspondence. That is the failure mode to watch for, and it announces itself as "I'll do the build next week" said three times.

## Part Nine — When Things Go Wrong

*Nothing in this part is unusual. Every one of these has happened to everyone who has ever done this work, and each has a standard answer. Knowing the answer in advance is what stops a normal problem becoming a crisis in a week with an exam in it.*

### 31. The client disappears mid-project

You deliver the draft. Nothing. Two weeks pass, then four.

**Why it happens.** Almost never dissatisfaction. Usually the person who cared got busy, or a bigger fire started, or they are waiting on something internal they have not mentioned.

**What to do.** One email at two weeks, one at four, then stop and invoke the pause clause. Do not send a third. Do not build anything more.

~~~text
Hi [NAME],

The draft's still up at [URL] whenever you have a moment.

If now's a bad time that's completely fine — just let me know and
we'll pick it up when things are calmer. Per the agreement, if we
go past 60 days I'd need to requote to restart, so I'd rather
pause it deliberately than let it drift.
~~~

**Why the deposit saves you.** You have been paid something for the work done. The scope agreement says a stalled project is not refundable and restarting is requoted. Both of those exist precisely for this, which is the whole argument for having the page.

**The prevention.** A deposit and a named single approver. Projects with neither stall roughly half the time; projects with both stall rarely.

### 32. The supplier changes the catalogue format

Year two. You get the new PDF, run the pipeline, and it produces nonsense because the supplier redesigned everything.

**What it costs.** A rebuild of the extraction layer — most of the work of a first build, maybe eight hours instead of two.

**What to do.** Do the work and do not charge extra this time. Then, at renewal, adjust the fee upward and explain why plainly.

~~~text
[SUPPLIER] restructured the catalogue this year, so the update
took considerably longer than usual — I've absorbed it this time.
I'm adjusting next year's maintenance to €[X] to reflect that
these rebuilds happen every few years.
~~~

Absorbing it once buys enormous goodwill and costs six hours. Absorbing it silently forever is how a good retainer becomes a bad one.

### 33. The catalogue turns out to be scanned

You quoted, took a deposit, and then discovered the PDF is images of pages rather than text.

**This is your fault, and it is preventable.** It is exactly what the twenty-minute check before quoting exists to catch. Add it to the pre-quote checklist and never skip it, no matter how confident you feel.

**If it has already happened.** Be straightforward and early:

~~~text
Now I'm into it properly, the catalogue turns out to be scanned
rather than digital text — which means the product data has to be
read out by OCR and checked by hand rather than extracted
directly. That's substantially more work than I priced.

Three options: I do it at €[X] instead of €[Y]; we do a reduced
version with the top [N] products at the original price; or I
return the deposit and we leave it. Genuinely happy with any of
the three.
~~~

Offering to return the deposit is what makes this an honest conversation rather than a renegotiation under pressure. It is very rarely taken up.

### 34. The client wants to cancel the maintenance

Year two or three, they decide the annual fee is not worth it.

**Do not argue and do not discount.** Both damage a relationship worth more than the fee.

~~~text
No problem at all. The site keeps working exactly as it is — it
just won't be updated when the catalogue changes.

If you want the update done later as a one-off, it's €[X].
Everything's archived, so picking it back up is straightforward.
~~~

The one-off price should be meaningfully higher than the annual fee — around double. That is not punitive, it is accurate: an ad-hoc update means reloading a project you have not touched in eighteen months.

Roughly half of clients who cancel come back within two years, usually when a customer complains about wrong product information. Leave the door wide open.

### 35. School gets in the way

Exams, a project deadline, a bad term. Your four hours become zero for a month.

**This is the one to plan for**, because it is certain rather than possible.

**Before it happens.** Do not start a build in the eight weeks before major exams. Look at the school year once, mark the impossible periods, and simply do not sign anything that would deliver into them. A client told in advance that you deliver in May rather than March will almost always wait; a client told in March that you have missed the date will not forgive it.

**When it happens anyway.** Tell them before the date slips, not after:

~~~text
Heads up — I've got exams through [DATES] and I'd rather move the
delivery date to [NEW DATE] than rush it. Nothing's wrong with the
project, I just don't want to hand you something half-finished.

If that date's a problem for you, tell me now and we'll work
something out.
~~~

Missing a date you announced in advance is a scheduling adjustment. Missing a date you announced after it passed is unreliability. The two are experienced completely differently and the only difference is timing.

### 36. The site breaks

A domain expires, a host changes something, a supplier's datasheet URLs move.

**Have this ready before it happens.** A file, per client, with: where it is hosted, where the domain is registered and when it renews, where the source data lives, and the deployment steps. Ten minutes per client, once.

**When it happens.** Acknowledge within your normal window, fix it, tell them what happened in one sentence. Do not over-apologise — a static site breaking is rare and usually external, and treating it as a catastrophe teaches the client that it was one.

**Domain renewals are the commonest cause and the most embarrassing.** Put every client's renewal date in a calendar with a reminder six weeks ahead, the day the site goes live. It is covered by the maintenance fee, so paying it is your job.

### 37. Someone copies you

You find a competitor offering catalogue sites in the same niche.

**This is good news.** It means the market is real. A market with no competition is usually a market with no customers.

**What to actually do.** Nothing defensive. Compete on the two things they cannot easily copy: your existing client relationships, and the fact that you have run the pipeline enough times to be fast. Do not lower prices — that is the one response that damages you more than the competitor.

### 38. You lose interest

The likeliest failure of all, and the one nobody writes about.

**It shows up as** the Saturday block quietly not happening. Three weeks running, and then it is a thing you used to do.

**What actually helps.** Not discipline — a smaller commitment. Reduce to maintenance only: keep the existing clients, do the annual updates, stop prospecting. That is a few hours a year and it keeps the income and the option open.

Shutting down entirely means telling clients, archiving everything, and losing the annuity. Dropping to maintenance costs almost nothing and can be reversed in an afternoon whenever interest returns.

> **The thing worth remembering**
>
> A business that earns €2,000 a year on ten hours of work is not a failed version of a bigger one. It is a good outcome that happens to be small, and small businesses that keep running quietly beat ambitious ones that stop.

## Part Ten — The Shop, in Maintenance Mode

### 39. Why power strips are structurally hard

Nothing here is a criticism of the shop. It works, money is deployed in it, and it taught you things about pricing and logistics that most people your age have never encountered. The question is only where your **scarce hours** go, and the answer is that they should mostly not go here.

#### The four properties that make this category difficult

**The product is identical everywhere.** A Brennenstuhl extension lead is the same object regardless of who ships it. There is no version of it that is yours. This removes every lever except price.

**The buyer sorts by price.** That is what a comparison engine is for. Your listing appears next to ten others, ordered by the number that most disadvantages the smallest seller.

**Your competitors buy better than you.** Public, Plaisio and Kotsovolos purchase at volumes that get them a wholesale price you cannot access. In a pure price race, the person with the lowest cost base wins, and it will not be you.

**The category is transparent.** Everyone can see everyone's price at all times, so margin gets competed down to nearly nothing and stays there.

> **The structural summary**
>
> Identical products, price-sorted buyers, competitors with better wholesale, and total price transparency. That is the definition of a commodity market, and commodity markets pay the operator with the lowest cost, not the one who works hardest.

#### What this does and does not imply

It does **not** imply closing the shop. There is inventory, there are established listings, there is working process. That has real value and abandoning it would be destroying something functional in pursuit of something speculative.

It implies **maintenance mode**: keep it running on minimum hours, stop trying to grow it with time, and make only the one adjustment described in the next chapter — which is about what you stock, not how hard you work.

### 40. The one adjustment worth making

Your edge on Skroutz is not buying power and never will be. Your edge is that you built analysis tooling for the platform, which means you can see the market in a way most small sellers cannot.

Point that tooling at a different question. Not "what price should I set on this power strip," but **"which categories are structurally less awful than the one I am in."**

#### What a better category looks like

| Signal | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Few sellers per product | Fewer people to be undercut by |
| Wide price spread between sellers | The market is not yet efficient — margin exists |
| Low review counts | Big players have not moved in |
| Products with specifications | Buyers filter on fit, not only price |
| Fragmented or many-variant range | Hard for large sellers to stock completely |
| Bulky or awkward to ship | Deters competitors; a genuine barrier |
| Rarely discounted | Stable pricing rather than a race |

*Seven signals. Anything scoring five or more deserves a closer look.*

#### What a bad category looks like

The inverse, and it is worth naming plainly because it describes where you currently are: many sellers per product, tight price clustering, high review counts, no meaningful specification differences, small light easily-shipped items, and constant discounting.

#### How to run the check

You already have the harder half of this built. The analysis you need is:

1. Pull listings across a set of candidate categories
2. For each product, count sellers and compute the spread between lowest and highest price
3. Rank categories by median spread, then filter to those with few sellers
4. Manually inspect the top ten for whether they are plausible to source

Run it once. It is a few hours of work, it produces a shortlist that stays useful for a year, and it is the only Skroutz project worth taking hours from the web business for.

> **The realistic expectation**
>
> This does not turn the shop into a great business. It moves it from a very hard category to a moderately hard one, which might mean better margin per unit on the same effort. That is worth a few hours once. It is not worth restructuring your life around, and it is not where the growth is.

### 41. Maintenance mode, defined

| Activity | Frequency | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Process orders | As they arrive | Unavoidable |
| Restock | Batched, monthly | 30 min |
| Price check on top sellers | Monthly | 20 min |
| Everything else | Never | — |

*Under an hour a month, outside order handling. That is the target.*

The discipline is in the last row. Do not optimise listings weekly. Do not chase every price change. Do not add products speculatively. The shop is a stable, modest income stream and your attention adds very little to it, because the variables that determine its performance are mostly outside your control.

#### Capital, not time

Here is the useful way to think about the shop: it is a **capital allocation** decision, not a time allocation one. Money sits in inventory and produces a return. The question is whether that money would produce a better return somewhere else.

For now the answer is probably no — the web business needs almost no capital, so there is nothing better to move it to. That means leaving the shop capitalised as it is and simply not spending hours on it is the correct call. If the web business ever needs money (it will not need much), the shop is where it comes from.

### 43. What to do with it

Not financial advice — just three practical observations about money in a business this size.

#### Keep a float

Leave enough in the business to cover a year of hosting for every client, plus a small buffer. A few hundred euros. The point is that a hosting renewal never becomes a decision, because a client's site going down over an unpaid €40 renewal is the kind of failure that ends a relationship worth thousands.

#### Buy tools only when the tool is the constraint

You have been looking at a MacBook Air. It is a genuinely good machine and you will eventually want one. The test for whether to buy it now is precise: **is the current machine the reason you are not earning more?**

At four hours a week the honest answer is almost certainly no. Your constraint is hours and clients, not compute. A faster laptop does not produce a client. If you buy it out of the first €1,500, you have converted a year of the business into a purchase and started again from zero.

The better sequence: let the business pay for it out of income you are not otherwise using, once the maintenance line covers it annually. That way the machine is bought by the business rather than by the business's entire existence. It also makes it a much more satisfying purchase.

> **The reframe that helps**
>
> Money in this business is not a wage. It is fuel and evidence. Fuel to keep going, evidence that the thing works. The first thousand euros is worth far more as proof than as a purchase — because what it proves is that you can do this again.

#### Do not scale spending with revenue

The trap that kills small operations: earning €300 and immediately committing to €50 a month of subscriptions, tools and services that felt justified at the time. Your cost base should stay near zero for a long time. Hosting a static site costs almost nothing, and almost everything else you might buy is a substitute for hours you do not have anyway.

### 44. Things not to do

A short list, each of which will be suggested to you by somebody.

**Do not build a product before you have sold a service.** A tool, an app, a SaaS — all enormously appealing and all requiring hundreds of unpaid hours before the first euro. You have four hours a week. Services pay from the first delivery.

**Do not take equity instead of payment.** "We can't pay much but you'll get a share" is a way of paying you nothing for certain instead of something for certain.

**Do not do free work to build a portfolio.** You already have a portfolio piece. One is enough. Free work attracts clients who value the work at zero, which is what they paid.

**Do not learn a new framework for a client project.** The temptation is real and the cost is enormous at your budget. Plain HTML and vanilla JS is what you are fast in, and fast is the entire economic argument for this business. Learn new things on your own projects, on your own time.

**Do not take a client outside the niche because they asked nicely.** The first restaurant website will take four times as long as you think, because none of your pipeline applies. Refer them to someone and stay narrow.

**Do not chase anything promising passive income.** Dropshipping, print-on-demand, affiliate schemes, trading of any kind. Each one is either a business requiring more hours than you have, or a way of transferring your money to whoever sold you the idea.

**Do not tell yourself you will do it next week, three weeks running.** That is the actual failure mode. Not competition, not pricing, not clients. Just the block quietly not happening until the whole thing is a thing you used to be doing.

### H. Questions you will ask yourself

##### Should I lower my price to get the first client faster?
No. The first client sets the reference for every subsequent quote, and a cheap first client generates a cheap referral. If nothing lands, the problem is nearly always the number of prospects contacted, not the price.

##### What if I can't finish something I've promised?
Say so before the date, offer a new date, and hold that one. Almost all client anger about lateness is anger about being told late.

##### Should I learn [framework] to make this better?
No. You are fast in plain HTML and vanilla JS, and speed is the entire economic argument for this business. Learn new things on your own projects, not on paid work with a deadline.

##### Is it dishonest not to mention my age?
No. You are not asked your age on any professional engagement and it does not affect the work. If asked, answer plainly. Volunteering it invites a decision on the wrong criterion.

##### What if a client asks for a company or a VAT number?
Tell them the truth: you are paid by τίτλος κτήσης, their accountant issues it. Most accountants deal with this routinely. If a specific client's system genuinely cannot handle it, that client is not for you and there are others.

##### Should I build a website for my own business?
Eventually, and it matters much less than you think. Clients in this sector are convinced by a live example of a site you built for someone else, not by your own. A single well-made case study page is enough for years.

##### What if the client's supplier gives them a website for free?
Then that client is gone, and it happens occasionally. Supplier-provided sites are usually generic, cover only that supplier's products, and cannot be customised — which is exactly the pitch to a distributor carrying three brands.

##### How do I know if I'm charging too little?
If nobody ever hesitates at your price, it is too low. A healthy rate is one where roughly a quarter of prospects find it too expensive. Zero objections means you are leaving money on the table on every single deal.

##### Is two clients a business?
Yes, at this time budget. Two clients paying maintenance is a functioning annuity. The instinct to feel it should be twenty is imported from businesses with twenty times your available hours.

##### What if this all takes longer than the plan says?
It will. Every plan in this document is optimistic by roughly a third, which is the standard error rate for all plans everywhere. Multiply the timeline by 1.5 and it will be about right, and being right about that in advance is worth more than the original estimate.

### I. The case study page

*Referenced in Appendix A, week 3. This is the single most important marketing asset you will make, and it is one page. Every outreach email links to it, and it does more persuading than anything you write.*

#### Why one page beats a portfolio site

A portfolio implies breadth and invites comparison across many pieces. You do not have breadth and do not need it. What you need is one piece of evidence that the exact thing you are selling exists and works.

A prospect clicking your link is asking one question: **has this person done this before, for someone like me?** A single page answering yes, with a live link, answers it completely. Ten pages of unrelated work answers it worse.

### J. When to widen the niche

Staying narrow is right at the start and wrong forever. This is the test for when to move.

#### The rule

**Do not widen until you have sold the same product three times.** Not two. Three, because two can be luck and three means a repeatable process.

Widening before then means you have two half-built pipelines instead of one working one, and at four hours a week you cannot afford a second learning curve.

#### The three directions

**Sideways — an adjacent sector.** Workwear to tools to fasteners. Same product, same pitch, same pipeline; only the field map changes. This is the cheapest expansion and should be the first.

**Upward — larger clients.** A distributor with forty employees and three supplier catalogues rather than one. Same work, roughly triple the price, more relationship overhead. Worth doing once the process is smooth enough that overhead is your binding constraint rather than build time.

**Outward — a second product.** Something other than catalogue sites. This is by far the most expensive direction and should be last, if ever.

| Direction | Cost to you | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sideways | Very low | After three sales |
| Upward | Low | After five sales |
| Outward | High | Probably never, at this time budget |

*The costs are asymmetric, and the ordering matters more than the timing.*

#### Signals that it is time

- You have sold three and the third took noticeably less effort than the second
- You are turning away work that is nearly what you do
- Your prospect list in the current niche is running out
- A client asks whether you could do the same for a different sector

The last one is the best signal of all, because it comes with a client attached.

#### Signals that it is not

- You are bored
- You saw something more interesting online
- The current niche feels small
- One prospect said no

Boredom is the commonest reason people widen too early, and it is the worst one. A narrow business that is slightly dull and pays reliably is worth considerably more than an interesting one that never quite starts.

### L. The five numbers to track

*Not a dashboard. Five numbers, updated once a month, in the same spreadsheet as your payments. Their only job is to tell you which part of the machine is broken when nothing is happening.*

#### The numbers

| Number | How to read it |
| --- | --- |
| Prospects contacted this month | Below 10 is the usual reason for silence |
| Replies received | Under 5% means the email is wrong, not the market |
| Proposals sent | Replies that never become proposals means you are not asking |
| Clients won | The only one that pays |
| Hours worked | Sanity check against the four-hour budget |

#### Diagnosing from them

The value of tracking is that failure has a location. When a month passes with nothing, one of these five tells you where.

**Contacted is low.** By far the commonest problem and the easiest to fix. You did not send enough emails. Ten a week for three weeks is the minimum that produces anything, and most people who conclude "this doesn't work" sent nine in total.

**Contacted is fine, replies are near zero.** The email is the problem. Check three things in order: does the first line name their actual catalogue, is it under 120 words, and are the prospects genuinely scoring four or more on the qualifying sheet. It is nearly always the first.

**Replies are fine, proposals are few.** You are having conversations and not asking for the sale. The fix is mechanical: any reply showing interest gets a proposal PDF within the week, not more conversation.

**Proposals are fine, wins are zero.** Either the price is far off, or you are quoting people who cannot buy. Three or four proposals declined on price is a signal; one is not. Do not adjust anything after a single no.

**Hours are above budget.** Scope creep, or you took on something outside the niche. Look at where the hours went — it is almost always revisions on one client, and that client is the one who did not get a scope agreement.

> **The trap this prevents**
>
> Without these numbers, a quiet month feels like "this isn't working," which is a conclusion about the whole business. With them, it is "I sent four emails," which is a conclusion about last month. The first one makes people stop; the second one makes them send more emails.

#### The monthly review

Fifteen minutes, once a month, at the end of a Saturday block:

1. Fill in the five numbers.
2. Read the one that is worst and name the fix in one sentence.
3. Check the running total against the €10,000 ceiling.
4. Check nothing is due to renew that you have forgotten.

That is the entire management overhead of this business.

### Closing note

Almost everything in this document is detail. Strip it away and four things remain, and if you only ever do these four, the rest will mostly take care of itself.

---

**One.** Sort out how you get paid. It is boring, it takes an afternoon, and until it is done every other hour you spend is unmonetisable. It is the only thing in this document that is currently, actually blocking you.

**Two.** Put the maintenance fee in the same price block as the build. One line in one document, decided once, worth more over three years than any other decision here.

**Three.** Write the scope down before you start, every time, including the time it feels unnecessary because the client seems nice. It is not about the client being difficult. It is about a four-hour week having no slack in it.

**Four.** Protect the Saturday block. The business is not the emails or the plans or this document. It is the hours where the work actually happens, and they only happen if something defends them.

---

The rest is repetition. Find a distributor with a catalogue problem, solve it, charge properly, attach a fee, and do it again. Six times over three years is a real business built on two hundred hours a year — which is the same amount of time most people spend deciding what to watch.

> **One last honest word**
>
> The numbers in this document are modest on purpose. A few thousand euros a year is what four hours a week produces, and it is a genuinely good outcome for those hours. But the money is not the main thing you get. At the end of three years you will have run a business end to end — priced work, written contracts, handled clients, been paid, filed the paperwork. That is an unusual thing to have done, and it compounds in ways a bigger number in year one would not.
